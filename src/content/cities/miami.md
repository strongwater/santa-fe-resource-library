<section class="sub" style="padding-top:8px;">
<div class="section-head"><div class="section-num">THE APPROACH</div><h2 class="section-title">Preserving the recent past, with a market-based incentive</h2></div>
<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card">
<p>The MiMo (Miami Modern) Biscayne Boulevard Historic District, designated in 2006, protects 1950s–60s roadside and motel-strip commercial architecture — a genuinely less conventional preservation target than the 19th-century residential fabric most historic districts protect, spanning a period of significance from 1923 to 1967.</p>
<p>The district pairs its design guidelines, including a 35-foot height limit, with a market-based tool: owners of historic buildings in eligible areas can sell their unused base development rights — the gap between a historic building's existing square footage and what current zoning would otherwise allow — to developers seeking extra density nearby, letting historic buildings monetize their own restriction rather than only bear its cost.</p>
<div class="stat-row">
<span class="stat">period of significance <b>1923–1967</b></span>
<span class="stat">district height capped at <b>35 ft</b></span>
</div>
<p class="muted">The Vagabond Motel, built in the 1950s, was restored and reopened as the Vagabond Hotel in 2014, preserving its original terrazzo flooring and landmark sign — a flagship adaptive-reuse example. Later proposals to raise the district's 35-foot height limit to 55 feet drew organized resident opposition, a common tension in incentive-based preservation: once a height ceiling becomes negotiable for one project, neighbors often lose confidence it will hold as a real limit.</p>
