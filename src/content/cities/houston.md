<div class="section-head"><div class="section-num">THE INCENTIVE</div><h2 class="section-title">What Houston did</h2></div>
<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card">
<p>In 1998, Houston reduced the minimum lot size inside the I-610 Loop from 5,000 to 3,500 sq ft by-right, and allowed lots as small as 1,400 sq ft when developers included compensating open space. In 2013, City Council extended a similar reform citywide and added a shared-driveway townhouse option that fits roughly 50% more units on a given parcel than the original open-space model.</p>
<div class="stat-row">
<span class="stat"><b>~25,000</b> new residential parcels under 5,000 sq ft, 1999–2016</span>
<span class="stat"><b>27%</b> of new Inner Loop construction since 2005 is townhomes</span>
<span class="stat">Density in treated neighborhoods <b>more than doubled</b>, 2000–2016</span>
</div>
<p class="muted">This is a density and production success story, not a walkability one. Houston's citywide Walk Score remains in car-dependent territory — the reform unlocked compact infill in specific inner neighborhoods, but didn't on its own create the walkable, transit-connected, low-car-dependence pattern Santa Fe is aiming for. Unlocking compact infill and building a genuinely walkable neighborhood are two different jobs, and Houston is a clean example of the first without the second.</p>
