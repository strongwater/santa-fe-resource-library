<div class="section-head"><div class="section-num">THE APPROACH</div><h2 class="section-title">The first historic-district ordinance in the U.S.</h2></div>
<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card">
<p>Charleston's Old and Historic District, created by ordinance in 1931, is generally credited as the first historic-district zoning ordinance in the United States. The Board of Architectural Review (BAR) it established had no precedent to follow — the design-review model it built (a board with Certificate-of-Appropriateness-style approval) became the template copied nationwide, rather than an application of an existing tool.</p>
<p>The BAR's authority was tested and held during Hurricane Hugo recovery in 1989, when the board unanimously declined to relax standards for storm-damaged buildings. Separately, Historic Charleston Foundation ran a revolving-fund and preservation-easement rehabilitation program in the Ansonborough neighborhood starting in 1959 — pairing regulatory protection with a market-based rehab mechanism a full generation before that became a common preservation-finance model elsewhere.</p>
<div class="stat-row">
<span class="stat">district protects more than <b>4,800</b> historic structures today</span>
<span class="stat">1944 survey: <b>1,168</b> buildings; 1974 survey: <b>2,800+</b></span>
<span class="stat">1967 expansion roughly <b>tripled</b> the district's size</span>
</div>
<p class="muted">Charleston is a frequently cited case study of preservation-driven tourism pressure — the peninsula's residential population declined for decades before recently beginning to recover, a trend local reporting links partly to tourism and short-term-rental pressure inside the historic core.</p>
