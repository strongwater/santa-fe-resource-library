<div class="section-head"><div class="section-num">HISTORIC PRESERVATION</div><h2 class="section-title">An automatic incentive, and a governance gap</h2></div>
<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card">
<p>The Charlotte-Mecklenburg Historic Landmarks Commission, created in 1973 as a joint city-county body, is one of the oldest local preservation commissions in this library’s set — and unusual for spanning both a city and its surrounding county rather than city limits alone. Landmark designation triggers an automatic 50% deferral of property taxes: self-executing, with no separate application or competitive award, alongside Certificate of Appropriateness design review and up to a one-year delay on any demolition request. If a landmarked property is later de-designated, the deferred taxes become repayable — discouraging owners from seeking the tax benefit only to abandon designation afterward.</p>
<div class="stat-row">
<span class="stat"><b>472</b> properties currently hold landmark designation</span>
</div>
<p class="muted">A Charlotte Urban Institute review found that 37% of landmarked or surveyed historic properties had been demolished or significantly altered over time regardless of designation; in the Fourth Ward, only 4 of 27 original blocks retain their historic building stock. In 2024, the Historic Landmarks Commission recommended landmark status for the Steele Creek Manse, but city staff never forwarded that recommendation to Council for a vote — the building was demolished before it received the protection its own review board had recommended. The incident points to a governance gap rather than a flaw in the incentive itself: an automatic tax deferral only protects a property once it is actually designated, and a process step where a commission’s recommendation can go unenacted without a formal denial leaves that protection incomplete.</p>

<section class="sub">
<div class="section-head"><div class="section-num">GENERAL PLAN LANGUAGE</div><h2 class="section-title">In the plan's own words</h2></div>
<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card">
<div class="quote-stack">
<p class="quote"><b>Vision</b>"Charlotte is America's Queen City, opening her arms to a diverse and inclusive community of residents, businesses, and visitors alike; a safe family-oriented city where people work together to help everyone thrive."</p>
<p class="quote"><b>Goal 1 — 10-Minute Neighborhoods</b>"All Charlotte households should have access to essential amenities, goods, and services within a comfortable, tree-shaded 10-minute walk, bike, or transit trip by 2040," measured against nine named amenities.</p>
<p class="quote"><b>Place Types → code linkage</b>"These Place Types will in turn provide the policy-level guidance that will inform the City's Unified Development Ordinance (UDO)... Each Place Type will correspond with multiple zoning districts... on items such as height, lot size, setbacks, adjacencies, and allowed uses."</p>
<p class="quote"><b>Goal 2, Policy 1 (missing middle)</b>"Allow single-family, duplex, and triplex housing units, as well as small footprint homes and accessory dwelling units (ADUs), in neighborhood Place Types."</p>
</div>
<p>The plan states outright, in its own text, that its Place Types will map to future zoning districts — the plan-to-code linkage is written as a commitment, not left implicit. "10-Minute Neighborhood" is dated, quantified, and publicly legible.</p>
<p class="borrow"><b>Borrow:</b> state explicitly that land-use designations will map one-to-one onto future LDC zoning districts; adopt a single testable walkability standard as the plan's signature commitment.</p>
