<div class="section-head"><div class="section-num">THE INCENTIVE</div><h2 class="section-title">How California's toolkit works</h2></div>
<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card">
<p>The State Density Bonus Law (Gov. Code §65915) gives developers extra height, density, and reduced parking by-right in exchange for including affordable units — no rezoning or discretionary hearing required. A parallel series of state ADU laws (2016–2023) made backyard and internal accessory units nearly by-right statewide, with fee waivers and short mandatory review timelines. SB 35 (2017) added ministerial, no-hearing approval for infill projects that meet objective zoning and affordability standards.</p>
<div class="stat-row">
<span class="stat"><b>140,000+</b> homes enabled by the density bonus law, 69,000+ affordable</span>
<span class="stat"><b>15,334%</b> growth in ADU permits, 2016–2022</span>
<span class="stat">ADUs are now <b>19%</b> of all new California housing units (2022)</span>
<span class="stat"><b>18,000+</b> units approved via SB 35 before its 2025 sunset, extended by SB 423</span>
</div>
<p class="muted">Not every enabling law gets used at this scale: SB 9's parallel duplex/lot-split law saw only 266 permits statewide as of 2023 against 6.1 million eligible parcels — a reminder that legal permission alone doesn't guarantee uptake if the underlying economics don't pencil out for a typical property owner.</p>

<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card">
<p><a href="/cities/berkeley/"><strong>Berkeley</strong></a> — the site of <em>Ruegg &amp; Ellsworth v. City of Berkeley</em> (2021), the first appellate case testing SB 35, which forced the city to ministerially approve a 260-unit mixed-use project it had tried to block on historic-preservation grounds.</p>
</article>
<article class="card">
<p><a href="/cities/santabarbara/"><strong>Santa Barbara</strong></a> pairs the statewide framework with its own Average Unit-Size Density (AUD) Incentive Program — allowing up to 63 units/acre in exchange for keeping units near 1,000 sq ft, letting a historic-scaled building hold more homes without changing its footprint.</p>
</article>
