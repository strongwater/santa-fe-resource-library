<div class="section-head"><div class="section-num">THE ZONING REFORM</div><h2 class="section-title">What happened in Gainesville</h2></div>
<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card clay">
<p>Eliminated single-family zoning citywide in 2022; a change in council composition brought most of it back in 2023 — a clear illustration of why anchoring a reform in the comprehensive plan, with durable public buy-in, helps it last.</p>

<section class="sub">
<div class="section-head"><div class="section-num">GENERAL PLAN LANGUAGE</div><h2 class="section-title">In the plan's own words</h2></div>
<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card clay">
<div class="quote-stack">
<p class="quote"><b>Goal 1</b>"Improve the quality of life and achieve a superior, sustainable development pattern in the city by creating and maintaining choices in housing... within walking distance of important destinations."</p>
<p class="quote"><b>Mixed-Use Low-Intensity category</b>Permits "small-lot single-family houses, duplex houses, townhouses (attached housing), accessory dwelling units, group homes, multi-family housing" — language still in the plan today, in 2026.</p>
</div>
<div class="note"><b>Why this one matters most:</b> the 2022 reform and its 2023 reversal both lived in the Land Development Code and zoning-district text — not in the comprehensive plan, whose permissive categories were untouched by either change. The lesson: an allowance anchored in comp-plan policy (which in Florida requires a state transmittal/review process) carries the kind of durability friction that helps a reform last.</div>
<p class="borrow"><b>Borrow — this is the case for doing GP + LDC together:</b> an upzoning that never touches the comprehensive plan is exactly as durable as the next election. Anchor missing-middle allowances in adopted comp-plan policy, not zoning-code text alone.</p>
