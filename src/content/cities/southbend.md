<div class="section-head"><div class="section-num">THE ZONING REFORM</div><h2 class="section-title">What South Bend did</h2></div>
<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card">
<p>A smaller, lower-cost-of-living city that took a different tack: instead of only rewriting zoning code, it pre-approved a catalog of missing-middle and small-multifamily building designs, so builders skip a chunk of design and permitting friction. Indianapolis has since copied the approach.</p>

<section class="sub">
<div class="section-head"><div class="section-num">GENERAL PLAN LANGUAGE</div><h2 class="section-title">In the plan's own words</h2></div>
<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card">
<div class="quote-stack">
<p class="quote"><b>Vision</b>"A Shared Vision for South Bend 2045" — connecting "people to each other, to place and to opportunity."</p>
<p class="quote"><b>Growth policy</b>Prioritize "density and infill within the city limits" over outward expansion.</p>
<p class="quote"><b>Catalog purpose</b>"The City of South Bend seeks to support neighborhood infill... by offering a set of pre-approved building types," addressing "a growing mismatch between the available housing stock of large single-family homes and a shift in demographic trends towards smaller households that prefer walkable neighborhoods."</p>
</div>
<div class="note"><b>Honest note:</b> South Bend doesn't yet have a fully adopted new comprehensive plan — the 2045 process is still underway. Its most actionable artifact is the infill catalog, a literal buildable deliverable rather than plan language. And the parallel to Santa Fe is imperfect: South Bend built this to fight population loss and cheap land by making infill fast and free to design, while Santa Fe's binding constraint is the opposite — high land/construction costs plus historic-district review.</div>
<p class="borrow"><b>Borrow the model, adapt the target:</b> a ready-to-permit pattern book scaled to context turns "increase housing diversity" into something a builder can pull a permit against — a Santa Fe version would need to solve for cost and historic-preservation compatibility, not just permitting speed.</p>
