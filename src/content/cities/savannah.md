<section class="sub" style="padding-top:8px;">
<div class="section-head"><div class="section-num">THE APPROACH</div><h2 class="section-title">A revolving fund, not a government incentive</h2></div>
<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card">
<p>Historic Savannah Foundation's Revolving Fund is a nonprofit mechanism, not a city ordinance — itself the notable structural point, since it's market-based preservation finance operating alongside the city's historic-district zoning rather than only government regulation.</p>
<p>The Foundation buys vacant, blighted, or endangered historic buildings with privately raised capital, stabilizes them, then resells them through a competitive process to preservation-minded buyers with a permanent preservation easement attached to the deed at sale. Proceeds from each sale recycle back into the fund to buy the next building, converting one-time philanthropic capital into a continuously renewing acquisition tool — a model later copied nationally.</p>
<div class="stat-row">
<span class="stat"><b>420</b> buildings saved and protected since the fund's inception</span>
</div>
<p class="muted">The National Park Service downgraded its assessment of Savannah's historic district in 2018, calling it the most threatened it had ever been, citing hotel development: 57 hotels and inns now operate within roughly one square mile of the historic district, with more planned, and the city drew 17 million visitors in 2022. Long-time residents report displacement pressure and quality-of-life strain — a well-funded, successful preservation program doesn't by itself resolve the tourism pressure that success can attract.</p>
