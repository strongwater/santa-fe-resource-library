<div class="section-head"><div class="section-num">THE INCENTIVE</div><h2 class="section-title">How Chapter 40B works</h2></div>
<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card">
<p>In any city or town where less than 10% of year-round housing is on the state's Subsidized Housing Inventory, a developer can apply for a single "comprehensive permit" from the local zoning board — one hearing instead of six or more separate local board approvals — in exchange for reserving 20–25% of units as long-term affordable (generally under 80% of area median income). A denial, or a condition that makes the project financially infeasible, can be appealed to the state Housing Appeals Committee, which can override the local decision. Towns that reach the 10% threshold gain "safe harbor" and may reject 40B applications without a state appeal.</p>
<div class="stat-row">
<span class="stat"><b>~70,000</b> homes produced statewide since 1969</span>
<span class="stat"><b>35,000+</b> of those income-restricted</span>
</div>
<p class="muted">It's a developer-side incentive and override mechanism, not a construction mandate on towns — no town is required to build, and towns retain veto power once they cross the 10% threshold. Voters upheld the law in a 2010 statewide repeal referendum. Massachusetts courts have narrowed its scope at the margins (a 2017 Supreme Judicial Court decision held it can waive zoning but not amend municipally-owned deed restrictions) without overturning the core mechanism.</p>

<section class="sub">
<div class="section-head"><div class="section-num">REPLICATED ELSEWHERE</div><h2 class="section-title">Three other states adopted the same model</h2></div>
<div class="card-grid">
<article class="card">
<p><strong>Connecticut (§8-30g, 1989):</strong> nearly identical structure — below 10% affordable stock, denials can be appealed to court with the burden shifted to the municipality to justify the denial.</p>
</article>
<article class="card">
<p><strong>Rhode Island:</strong> Low and Moderate Income Housing Act uses the same comprehensive-permit structure for towns below 10% (or 15% rental) affordable stock; reformed in 2024 to speed reviews.</p>
</article>
<article class="card">
<p><strong>Illinois (2004):</strong> the Affordable Housing Planning and Appeal Act was explicitly modeled on Chapter 40B, requiring housing plans and state appeals-board review below a 10% affordability threshold.</p>
</article>
</div>
